Title one of the ADA covers employment and applies to private employers with 15 or more employees, all state and local government employers, employment agencies, and labor unions. It does not apply to tribal organizations or federal agencies. Keep in mind that state law may impose employment access requirements on private employers with fewer employees.

Title one provides protection from discrimination and harassment based on disability in all employment practices such as job application, hiring, employment training, promotion, performance management, termination, and all other employment related activities. Employment discrimination oftentimes begin with the hiring process so that's why it's important to know and remember that the ADA prohibits all disability related inquiries prior to an offer of employment even if they are related to the job including the application. A job related inquiry is any question or series of questions that is likely to solicit information about a disability. After an applicant is given a conditional job offer but before they start working, an employer may make disability related inquiries and conduct medical examinations regardless of whether they're related to the job as long as the employer does so for all entering employees in the same job category. After employment begins an employer may make disability related inquiries and require medical examinations only if they are job related and consistent with business necessity.

Title one requires covered employers to provide reasonable accommodations to applicants and employees who have disabilities as long as these accommodations do not cause an undue hardship. Title one provides the right to be free from retaliation should an applicant or employee with a disability exercise their rights under the ADA. For example, an employer may not fire or discipline a person with a disability for requesting a reasonable accommodation.

The employment provisions are intended to provide an equal opportunity so that people with disabilities like anyone else can succeed as far as their talents and drive will take them. The employment provisions of the ADA are enforced by the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission otherwise known as EEOC. Moving on to slide three. The definition of reasonable accommodation stated on the slide is one put forth by the EEOC.

That is, quote, "any change in the work environment or in the way things are customarily done that enables an individual with a disability to enjoy equal employment opportunities," end quote. It is simply any change made to ensure that someone can fully contribute. The ADA provides reasonable accommodations to ensure people with disabilities have an equal opportunity to compete and be evaluated on their merits or worth, not their disability.

The purpose of reasonable accommodations is not to provide an advantage but to make the person's equal opportunity for success or failure equal to someone who does not have a disability. This is demonstrated by the equality versus equity image shown on this slide. On the left side of the image is an apple tree with three people of varying heights standing on the same size box and all reaching for an equally situated apple but only the tallest person can reach the apple.

On the right side of the image is the same apple tree and the same three people but this time the individuals are standing on different size boxes which illustrates reasonable accommodation and now all three individuals have the same opportunity to reach the apple regardless of their height. This is equity. Reasonable accommodations don't just apply to on-the-job functions. Job applicants also have the right to an accommodation to fully participate in the hiring process.

Under the ADA employers need to provide reasonable accommodations to address barriers created by the work environment, facilities, or policies, practices, or procedures. Reasonable accommodations also apply to benefits of employment. For example, accommodations must be provided to enable an employee to attend a training session or conference if this is a benefit that would be given to other employees in the same job class.

Moving on to slide four. The goal of reasonable accommodation is to keep an employee working in their current position. An employer is obligated to provide a reasonable and effective accommodation that address the barrier the employee is facing, not necessarily what the employee has specifically requested. For example, an employee with ADHD could request to work from home because the office environment is too noisy and distracting.

As an alternative, the employer could modify their policy to allow the employee to use an empty office room with a door rather than the standard cubicle. They could also provide a noise canceling headset rather than a headset model that is typically provided to staff. Accommodations must be reasonable that is feasible and plausible, which is assessed through an individual case-by-case basis.

If a particular accommodation would be an undue hardship, an employer must try to identify another accommodation that will not pose an undue hardship. Additionally, reasonable accommodation can be temporary for a designated period of time, such as while someone is undergoing medical treatment. Reasonable accommodations do not include elimination of an essential function. However, marginal functions can be removed or reassigned as an accommodation.

Reasonable accommodations do not include lowering production standards. It may be necessary to consider removing marginal job functions to free of time to enable an employee who is having difficulty completing documentation due to a disability-related reason to close the required number of cases in a timely manner, but the number of cases does not need to be reduced. Reasonable accommodations do not include transfer to a different supervisor.

An employer does not have to provide an employee with a new supervisor as a reasonable accommodation, but may need to implement other types of accommodations, like changes in supervisory methods, such as providing clear and written expectations and instructions. Reasonable accommodations do not include providing personal use items unless only needed at work. An employer is not required to provide an accommodation that is primarily for personal use.

However, personal use items may be required as a reasonable accommodation when they're only needed at work, such as computer glasses, hearing devices that only work with the company phone, cooling vests, specialized safety equipment, and so on. Another concept of reasonable accommodations is the ADA requirements do not prevent an employer from providing accommodations beyond what is required by the ADA.

The ADA covers state and local government and provides protections to qualified individuals with disabilities. Now, when you think about local government, that is your county government, your city or town government. It covers places and things like public education, public transportation, recreational programs, health care, social services, courts, voting, emergency services, libraries, renewing licenses, attending town hall meetings, and conducting other governed business or programs.

Title II prohibits disability discrimination and harassment. Title II requires effective communication, reasonable modifications, and program access. Effective communication and reasonable modifications will be discussed in the Americans with Disabilities Act requirements presentation. Title II of the ADA is enforced by the U.S. Department of Justice. Moving on to slide 7. A public entity may not deny the benefits of its programs, activities, and services to individuals with disabilities because its facilities are inaccessible.

A public entity's service programs or activities, when viewed in their entirety, must be readily accessible to and usable by individuals with disabilities. This standard is known as program access and applies to all existing facilities of a public entity. Public entities are not necessarily required to make each of their existing facilities accessible or every aspect of facilities structurally accessible.

This allows for both structural and non-structural methods of achieving program access. Examples of non-structural methods include reassigning services to accessible locations, home visits, purchase or redesign of equipment, or offering aid or assistance. Displayed on the slide is a photo of a healthcare worker kneeling down next to an elderly woman's wheelchair, and they are both holding and looking at a clipboard.

To evaluate the accessibility and usability of a library system, with six branches in its entirety, it's necessary to consider: 1. The resources and activities provided in each library and facility. 2. The operation of the libraries and the system as a network. 3. And 3. Any out-of-building activities that are part of the operation of the system. Answering these questions will determine whether all services and resources available in the system as a whole are available in accessible locations.

Physical changes to a building are required only when there is no other feasible way to make the program accessible. When structural change is the method chosen to provide program access, the alterations must comply with the 2010 ADA standards for accessible design, unless it's technically infeasible to do so. When full compliance is not technically feasible, the alteration must follow the standards to the maximum extent possible.

Moving on to slide 8. Let's look at a program access scenario. A state agency has five offices downtown. Two are accessible and three are not. If the same services and programs are available in all five locations, do the three inaccessible ones need to be made accessible? No. The agency does not have to make structural changes to those inaccessible offices as long as the accessible offices operate the same hours in businesses and services.

However, in a large city where there is a significant difference between facilities, this can create barriers to program access. Then structural changes may be necessary to achieve program access for everyone. Moving on to slide 9. There are administrative requirements under Title II, starting with all public entities providing a public notice to the public program participants, program beneficiaries, applicants, and employees about the ADA and how it applies to the public entity.

The notice needs to include the contact information of the responsible person to address access issues and needs and must be provided in alternative formats so it is accessible to people with hearing and vision disabilities. All state and local governments were required to complete a self-evaluation of their facilities, programs, policies, and practices by January 26, 1993. If they didn't, they must do so.

The self-evaluation identifies and corrects those policies and practices that are inconsistent with Title II requirements. Public entities with 50 or more full and part-time employees are also required to develop a transition plan if structural changes are necessary to achieve program access, designate at least one employee to oversee compliance, often known as the ADA coordinator, and establish a grievance procedure.

Moving on to slide 10. Title III of the ADA covers almost all types of businesses, including non-profits, that are open to the public where commerce is carried out, regardless of their size or the age of the buildings. Commerce happens when goods or services are bought or sold. This is known as public accommodation and includes places like restaurants, hotels, stores, private schools, daycare centers, doctor's offices, and private hospitals, and privately operated transit.

The overarching purpose of Title III is non-discrimination. Title III requires people with disabilities to be able to access all the types of goods and services that a business offers to a customer, which is achieved by providing effective communication, reasonable modifications, and removal of architectural barriers. Title III of the ADA is enforced by the U.S. Department of Justice.

Moving on to slide 11. All businesses are required to remove architectural barriers and existing facilities, even if their building were constructed prior to the passage of the ADA in 1990, when it is readily achievable, that is easy to do without much difficulty or expense. This is based on the business size and resources. The ADA strikes a careful balance between increasing access for people with disabilities and understanding the financial challenges of many small businesses, so a business with more resources is expected to remove more barriers than a business with fewer resources.

Readily achievable barrier removal generally refers to things like installing ramps, creating wider doorways, changing doorknobs from twist to lever action, creating designated accessible parking spots, and repositioning tables and shelves so that someone can easily move around as illustrated on this slide. When a public accommodation can demonstrate that the removal barrier is not readily achievable, the public accommodation must make its goods and services available through alternative methods, if such methods are readily achievable. For example, curb service, home delivery, relocation of activities to accessible locations, retrieve merchandise for inaccessible shelves or locations within a store.

A public accommodation cannot charge extra for these services. Moving on to slide 12. The ADA does not cover everything. All religious entity facilities, programs, and activities, including programs and activities open to the general public, whether they're religious or secular in nature, are exempt from the ADA, such as food banks, homeless shelters, fish stores, daycare centers, schools, hospitals, and so on. Tribal sovereignty renders the ADA largely and applicable to tribal nations.

Private membership clubs do not have to comply with the ADA regulations except when they are open their facilities to the general public. Courts in deciding whether an organization is truly a private club has considered factors such as the degree to which a member controls club operations, the selectivity of the membership process, whether substantial membership fees are charged, whether the organization is operated on a non-profit business basis, to what extent the facilities are open to the public and the club receives public funding, and whether the club was created specifically to avoid compliance with civil rights laws. Moving on to slide 13. Title IV of the ADA applies to telephone companies and public service announcements. Telephone companies must provide telecommunication relay services, or TRS, and must be functionally equivalent to standard telephone services. TRS enables telephone conversations between people with and without hearing or speech disabilities. Also entities that produce federally funded public service announcements must also add closed captioning to these announcements.

Title IV of the ADA is enforced by the Federal Communication Commission, or FCC. The FCC issued regulations specifying standards for the operation of these services. Moving on to slide 14.
