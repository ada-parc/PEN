Hello, and welcome to the Americans with Disabilities Act, ADA Requirements, moving on to slide 2. Entities covered under the ADA must follow the 2010 ADA Accessible Standards for Physical Accessibility and New Construction, Alterations, and Additions. Elements that have not been altered in existing facilities on or after March 15, 2012, and that comply with the corresponding technical and scoping specifications for those elements in the 1991 standards are not required to be modified in order to comply with the requirements set forth in the 2010 standards.

This is called Safe Harbor. New technical requirements for elements that were not addressed in the 1991 standards are not eligible for Safe Harbor, such as play areas, fishing piers, golf facilities, saunas, and so on. Grandfather provisions are often found in local building codes, but do not exempt businesses and state and local governments from their obligations under the ADA.

Covered entities must maintain, in working order, equipment and features of facilities that are required to provide ready access to individuals with disabilities. Required accessible routes must remain accessible and not blocked by obstacles such as furniture, filing cabinets, or potted plants. For example, using an accessible route for storage of supplies would be a violation if it made the route inaccessible.

An isolated instance of a placement of an object on an accessible route, however, would not be a violation if the object is promptly removed. Similarly, accessible doors must be unlocked when the public entity is opened for business. Mechanical failures in equipment, such as elevators or automatic doors, will occur from time to time. The obligation to ensure that facilities are readily accessible to and usable by individuals with disabilities would be violated if repairs are not made promptly, or if improper or inadequate maintenance causes repeated and persistent failures.

Moving on to slide 3. The ADA also applies to digital spaces such as websites and online services that state and local governments and businesses offer to the public. State and local governments must follow new web and mobile app accessibility requirements to ensure digital platforms are accessible. Some businesses choose to follow these new requirements to ensure their websites and online services are accessible, but are not obligated to do so.

In a 1996 letter from the Department of Justice in response to Senator Tom Harkin's question about the accessibility of web pages on the internet said, Covered entities under the ADA are required to provide effective communication, regardless of whether they generally communicate through print media, audio media, or computerized media such as the internet. Moving on to slide 4.

Covered entities must provide auxiliary aids and services when needed to communicate effectively with people who have communication disabilities at no extra cost. Effective communication is when a person with a vision, hearing, or speech disability can communicate with, receive information from, and convey information to the covered entity. For example, providing a sign language interpreter or real-time captioning for individuals who are deaf or have hearing loss, or providing a large print or accessible electronic copy of a document for individuals with vision loss or low vision.

Displayed on this slide are photos of tactile signing, two people signing, and a person using a communication pad. The communication must be provided in a manner that enables people with disabilities to participate in or benefit from the service or program on an equal basis with others, unless to do so would result in a fundamental alteration to the program or an undue financial administrative burden.

This requirement also applies to communicating with not just the person who is receiving the covered entity's good or services, but as well as that person's parent, spouse, or companion in appropriate circumstances. People with disabilities need to be given the opportunity to request auxiliary aid or service of their choice. State and local governments must honor that choice unless they can demonstrate that another equally effective means of communication is available or that the aid requested would result in a fundamental alteration or an undue burden.

Businesses are encouraged to honor the individual with a disability's choice, but effective communication must be the end result. Keep in mind that the individual with a disability is in the best position to determine what type of aid or service will be effective. State and local governments and businesses have formal and informal policies, practices, and procedures that keep them running smoothly.

However, sometimes these policies or procedures can inadvertently make it difficult or impossible for a customer with a disability to access goods and services. That is why the ADA requires covered entities to make reasonable modifications to their usual ways of doing things when serving people with disabilities. The purpose is not to provide an advantage, but to make a person with a disability's opportunity and access to the activities, programs, goods, and services equal to someone without a disability.

This is also demonstrated by the equality versus equity image described in the five titles of the ADA presentation when we discussed employment accommodations. There is no cookie-cutter approaches, and most modifications involve only minor adjustments in policies or practices. For example, a daycare center that has two scheduled snack times must modify this policy to allow a child with a diabetes to bring food for an extra snack if necessary.

A clothing store must modify a policy permitting only one person at a time in a dressing room for a person with a disability who is shopping with a companion and needs the companion's assistance to try and close. Other examples of reasonable modifications include permitting a service animal in a place where animals are typically not allowed, such as a cafeteria or courtroom, or granting a zoning variance to allow a ramp to be built inside a setback.

Covered entities are not required to provide the requested or preferred modification if they can provide an alternative that would be reasonable and effective. A modification may be appropriate under some circumstances, but not others. For example, a transcription service for a student with hearing loss may be appropriate in a lecture where the communication is generally one-sided.

If a student needs to participate in class discussions, a sign language interpreter may be more appropriate. Not all modifications will be the same for the same disability or limitation. Disabilities manifest itself differently in different people and individuals have their own coping mechanisms and responses to those limitations that they may experience. Some conditions can be episodic in nature and require different responses at different times.

Reasonable modification does not include tolerating or excusing violent or abusive behaviors or providing personal services such as toileting, feeding, clothing, and medication management, and so on. Personal services do not include minor assistance provided to individuals with disabilities such as retrieving items from shelves, providing curb service or home delivery, removing the cover from a customer's straw, a cook in a restaurant cutting up food into smaller pieces, or a bank teller filling out a deposit slip.

If a public accommodation, such as a hospital or nursing home, customarily provides clients with what might otherwise be considered services of a personal nature and must provide the same services for individuals with disabilities. Moving on to slide 6. Let's take a look at a modification scenario. A city ordinance states home owners must remove snow from their sidewalks within 48 hours.

A resident with a disability that inhibits their ability to shovel snow requests the city provide snow removal service as a reasonable modification. Is the city obligated to provide this? No. The ADA does not require a city government to provide snow removal service for the private driveways of residents with disabilities if the city does not provide such services for residents without disabilities.

Moving on to slide 7. Covered entities must allow service animals to go to most places where the public can go. A service animal is defined as a dog of any breed or size that has been individually trained to do work or perform tasks for an individual with a disability. Displayed on this slide is a photo of a service dog alerting a person of their blood sugar levels. The ADA does not require service animals to be professionally trained.

People with disabilities have the right to train a dog themselves and are not required to use a professional service dog training program. Covered entities must also modify policies to permit miniature horses as a service animal were reasonable. The ADA requires that service animals be under the control of the handler at all times. In most instances, the handler will be the individual with a disability or a third party who accompanies the individual with a disability.

In primary school and in similar settings, the school or similar entity may need to provide some assistance to enable a particular student to handle their service animal. The service animal must be harnessed, leashed, or tethered while in public places unless those devices interfere with the service animal's work or the person's disability prevents the use of these devices. In that case, the person must use voice, signal, or other effective means to maintain control of the animal.

For example, a person who uses a wheelchair may use a long, retractable leash to allow their service animal to pick up or retrieve items. The person may not allow the dog to wander away from them and must maintain control of the dog, even if it's retrieving an item at a distance from them. Or a returning veteran who has post-traumatic stress disorder and has great difficulty entering unfamiliar spaces may have a dog that is trained to enter a space, check to see that there are no threats in that space, and come back and signal that it is safe to enter.

Covered entities can also ask someone to remove their service animal if the dog is out of control and the person cannot get the dog under control or the dog is not housebroken. They may still allow the person with a disability to participate in the goods or services without the excluded service animal and may need to provide reasonable modifications and the absence of the person's service animal.

Moving on to slide eight. In situations where it's not obvious that a dog is a service animal covered, entity staff may ask only two questions. Is the dog a service animal required because of a disability? This is a yes or no response. If a person says yes to this first question, then a covered entity can follow up by asking, "What work or task has the dog been trained to perform?"

For example, someone might say, "My service animal alerts me to oncoming attacks that I may take precautionary measures to manage that attack." Entities cannot ask these questions if the disability and trained work or task are obvious. Staff are not allowed to request any documentation for the service animal, require that the service animal demonstrate its task, or inquire about the nature of a person's disability.

Service animals are not allowed to be certified or go through a professional training program, wear a vest or other ID that indicates they're a service animal. People with disabilities are not required to register their trained service animals, provide training documentation, carry a special ID card or certification or certificate, disclose their disability, or provide medical documentation of their disability.
