The ADA guarantees that people with disabilities have the same opportunities as everyone else to enjoy employment opportunities, purchase goods and services, and participate in programs, but does not guarantee that individuals with disabilities must achieve an identical result or level of achievement as persons without disabilities.

Individuals with disabilities cannot be excluded from the regular program. For example, a deaf person may not be excluded from a music concert because of a belief that deaf persons cannot enjoy music. A primary goal of the ADA is the equal participation of individuals with disabilities in the mainstream of American society. On passing the ADA, Congress recognized that historically, society has tended to isolate and segregate individuals with disabilities, and despite some improvements, such forms of discrimination against individuals with disabilities continue to be a serious and pervasive social problem.

The ADA bans the unnecessary segregation of people with disabilities and a regulation called the integration mandate. This means that state and local governments are required to administer services, programs, and activities in the most integrated setting appropriate to the needs of qualified individuals with disabilities. The Department of Justice has consistently interpreted the most integrated setting appropriate to mean one that enables individuals with disabilities to interact with non-disabled persons to the fullest extent possible.

In a 1999 case called Olmstead v. LC, the Supreme Court wrote that the unnecessary segregation of people with disabilities is a form of discrimination. It says that a person with a disability must be served in the community when community-based services are appropriate to the person's needs, the person does not oppose community-based services, and the state or local government entity can reasonably modify its programs and services to provide services in the community.

Community-based services are funded by federal, state, or local dollars, many times for Medicaid, to support people with disabilities living at home or in other community-based settings. A few examples of community-based services include nursing, personal services, mental health, medication management, peer mentorship, caregiver support such as training and respite, home modifications, and housing services that help individuals find, move into, and remain in community-based housing.

To comply with the ADA's integration mandate, public entities must make reasonable modifications to their policies, practices, or procedures when necessary to avoid discrimination on the basis of disability. The ADA protects people who have a disability or record of a disability and meet the essential job-related requirements for a position or eligibility requirements for the receipt of services or participation in programs. For example, the ADA must be 18 years old or older. Employees with disabilities must be qualified for a job. Employees with disabilities must be qualified for a job or have the skills to do the job and must be able to perform the essential functions of the job with or without reasonable accommodation.

Individuals need to meet the essential eligibility requirements for the receipt of services or participation in programs. For example, must be 18 years old or older. A person who has a record of a qualifying disability applies when there is a history of a debilitating illness such as mental illness, cancer, heart disease, and so on, and may be entitled to reasonable accommodation if the request relates to a past disability.

The ADA also protects people who are regarded as having a disability, whether or not they do. This part is meant to address issues of discrimination based on myths, fears, and stereotypes about disabilities. Individuals who are only covered under the regarded as part of the ADA are not entitled to accommodations. The ADA also protects people who have an association with a person with a disability.

For example, if a job applicant shares that their child has a disability, the employer cannot refuse to hire the applicant due to assumptions that the person will miss a lot of work to care for their child's disability. Take another example. A person who does volunteer work with people who have AIDS cannot be denied care by a doctor's office due to a fear that the person may contact, contract, and spread the disease to other patients.

So, what is an actual disability under the ADA? A disability under the ADA is a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities. This is a functional definition, not a medical one. Let's break down the terms in this definition. A physical impairment is any physiological disorder, condition, cosmetic disfigurement, or anatomical loss affecting one or more body systems.

A mental impairment is any mental or psychological disorder, such as intellectual disabilities, organic brain syndrome, emotional or mental illness, and specific learning disabilities. Major life activities are ones that are important to us in our daily lives, such as caring for oneself, seeing, hearing, eating, sleeping, walking, breathing, concentrating, thinking, communicating, interacting with others, and so on.

Major bodily functions are also considered to be major life activities, including things such as digestive, bowel, bladder, functions of the immune system, neurological, endocrine, cardiovascular, functions of the brain, and so on. For example, cancer significantly impacts normal cell growth and would meet the ADA definition of disability. When determining whether an impairment substantially limits a major life activity, the analysis is compared to most people and the general population and is applied broadly.

Additionally, the impairment's impact on the major life activity is evaluated without regard to mitigating or lessening measures such as medical treatment, medication, or assistive devices. A person's physical or mental condition does not need to be permanent or long-term to be substantially limiting. If symptoms come and go, what matters is how limiting they would be when the symptoms are present, such as in a flare-up.

Moving on to slide four. Certain things are not allowed under the ADA. Let's review three of them. Eligibility criteria intended to screen out people with disabilities as well as neutral criteria that has the result of screening out people with disabilities are prohibited unless the criteria are necessary for the provision of service, program, or activity. A neutral example would be a driver's license cannot be the sole form of photo identification accepted for securing a card at a public library.

The ADA prohibits unnecessary inquiries into the existence of disability for all covered entities. For example, a private or municipal Recreation Department summer camp requires parents to fill out a questionnaire and submit medical documentation regarding their children's ability to participate in various camp activities. The questionnaire is acceptable if the Recreation Department can demonstrate that each piece of information requested is needed to ensure safe participation in camp activities.

The Department, however, may not use this information to screen out children with disabilities from admittance to the camp. A covered entity cannot charge people with disabilities for providing them with ADA mandated access to goods and services because providing those is necessary to ensure people with disabilities have the same opportunity afforded to the general public to participate and benefit from goods and services.

For example, to ensure effective communication with a deaf patient during an office visit, a hospital arranges for the services of a sign language interpreter. The cost of the interpreter's services must be absorbed by the hospital. Moving on to slide 5. Let's take a look at an eligibility scenario. A county recreation program requires that all participants in its SCUBA program pass a swimming test.

Is this legal? Yes. If the county can demonstrate the ability that being able to swim is necessary for safe participation in the class. This is permitted even if requesting such a test would tend to screen out people with certain kinds of disabilities. Moving on to slide 6. ADA covered entities may impose legitimate safety requirements necessary for the safe operation of their services, programs, or activities.

However, covered entities must ensure that their safety requirements are based on actual risk, not on mere speculation, stereotypes, or generalizations about individuals with disabilities. Covered entities may exclude an individual with a disability from participation and activity if that individual's participation will result in a direct threat to the health or safety of others.

The ADA provides a framework for analyzing whether a person poses a direct threat to the safety of others, which requires a covered entity to conduct an individualized assessment or regional judgment that relies on current medical evidence or the best available objective evidence to determine the nature, duration, and severity of the potential harm. The likelihood that the potential harm will actually occur.

And whether reasonable modifications of policies, practices, or procedures will mitigate the risk to an acceptable level or eliminate the risk. Sources for medical evidence or knowledge include public health authorities, such as the U.S. Public Health Service, the Centers for Disease Control, and the National Institutes of Health, including the National Institute of Mental Health.

Such an assessment is essential to protect individuals with disabilities from discrimination based on prejudice, stereotypes, or unfounded fear, while giving appropriate weight to legitimate concerns, such as the need to avoid exposing others to significant health and safety risk. Covered entities do not need to take any action that would result in a fundamental alteration, which is a change that is so significant that it alters the essential nature of the goods, services, facilities, privileges, advantages, or accommodations offered.

Or would cause an action requiring significant difficulty or expense when considered in light of a number of factors such as the nature and cost of the accommodation, size, resources, nature, and structure of the entity's operation, not just a specific department's budget. This is a case-by-case determination. With regards to state and local governments, the undue burden determination can only be made by the head of the public entity or their designee and must be accompanied by a written statement of the reasons for reaching the conclusion.

Note, entities still need to find another way to ensure access to the greatest extent possible. Moving on to slide 8. The ADA requires covered entities to take several steps to become compliant with the ADA, such as: following the 2010 ADA accessible standards for physical accessibility when building or altering a building or facility; providing auxiliary aids and services to ensure effective communication; and making reasonable modifications to policies, practices, and procedures.
