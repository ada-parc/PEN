Moving on to slide three. Let's break down the Rehabilitation Act. Section 501 prohibits federal agencies from discriminating against qualified individuals with disabilities using the same standards as Title I of Employment of the ADA. It requires federal agencies to take affirmative action in hiring, placing, and advancing of individuals with disabilities. Section 501 is enforced by the Federal Agency's Equal Employment Opportunity Office.

Section 503 prohibits federal contractors and subcontractors from discriminating in employment against individuals with disabilities. It requires employers to take affirmative action to recruit, hire, promote, and retain individuals with disabilities. Section 503 is enforced by the Office of Federal Contract Compliance Programs within the U.S. Department of Labor. Section 504 of the Rehab Act states that no qualified individual with a disability in the U.S. shall be excluded from, denied the benefit of, or be subjected to discrimination under any program activity that either receives federal finance assistance or is conducted by any executive agency or the U.S. Postal Service.

Requirements common to these regulations include reasonable accommodations for employees with disabilities, program access, effective communication with people who have hearing or vision disabilities, and new construction and alterations built with accessibility in mind. Individuals can contact the U.S. Department of Justice for information on what appropriate federal agency to contact regarding the enforcement of Section 504.

Section 508 requires federal electronic and information technology to be accessible to people with disabilities, including employees and members of the public. It establishes requirements for electronic and information technology developed, maintained, procured, or used by the federal government. For more information on Section 508, individuals can contact the U.S. General Administration Office of Enterprise Planning and Governance.

Moving on to slide five. On this slide is a legislative timeline of civil rights laws providing protections to Americans with disabilities. Many of these laws provide protections that the ADA does not cover. Let's take a look at some of them. The Voting Rights Act was passed in 1965 and established a right for voters who are blind or have other disabilities to receive assistance from a person of their choice.

It also prohibited making the right to vote contingent on a citizen's ability to read or write, attaining a specific level of education, or passing an interpretation test. The Architecture Barriers Act of 1968 requires access to facilities designed, built, altered, or leased with federal funds or by federal agencies. It ensures accessibility in the built environment for people with disabilities through the Act's accessibility standards.

The Individuals with Disabilities Act of 1975, or IDEA, requires public schools to make available to all eligible children with disabilities a free, appropriate public education and the least restrictive environment appropriate to their individual needs. IDEA requires public school systems to develop appropriate individualized education programs, otherwise known as IEPs, for each child.

The Air Carry Access Act of 1986 prohibits discrimination on the basis of disability in air travel. The Department of Transportation, or DOT, has a rule defining the rights of passengers and the obligations of airlines under this law. This rule applies to all flights of the U.S. airlines into flights to or from the U.S. by foreign airlines. Department of Transportation's rule addresses prohibited discrimination practices.

For example, airlines may not require a person with a disability to travel with another person except in certain limited circumstances. The rule also addresses the accessibility of aircraft and other services and accommodations required by airlines. The Fair Housing Act, as amended in 1988, prohibits housing discrimination on the basis of race, color, religion, sex, disability, family status, and national origin.

The Fair Housing Act requires owners of housing facilities to make reasonable exceptions to their policies and operations to afford people with disabilities equal housing opportunities. It also requires landlords to allow tenants with disabilities to make reasonable access-related modifications to their private living space as well as common use spaces. The Act further requires that new multifamily housing with four or more units be designed and built to allow access for persons with disabilities by following the Fair Housing Act design and construction requirements.

This law is enforced by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development, otherwise known as HUD. In 1908, the ADA was further strengthened with the passage of the ADA Amendments Act in response to several Supreme Court decisions that weakened the law by placing narrow restrictions on who could be given protections of the law. When Congress passed the ADA Amendments Act, they made it clear that the original intent of the ADA was to provide broad coverage, that determining if a person has a disability as defined by the law should be straightforward, and that covered entities are to spend more time focusing on removal of disability-related barriers.

Additionally, it's important to note that the 21st Century Communications and Video Accessibility Act of 2010 updates federal communications law to increase the access of persons with disabilities to modern communications. This acts to make sure that accessibility laws enacted in the 1980s and 1990s are brought up to date with the 21st century technologies, including new digital, broadband, and mobile innovations.
